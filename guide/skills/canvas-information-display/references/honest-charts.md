# Honest charts

The same data can be drawn to say opposite things. Most of the ways to mislead with a chart are also done by accident: a default of a tool, a wish for a neat picture. This file names them, so that you do none of them without intent. To persuade with a picture is a different job from showing information; if the user asks for it, say plainly that the picture is no longer a display of the data.

## Numbers and a source

- **A chart is a picture of numbers.** With no numbers it means nothing: a curve that falls and rises with no values on its axes, two bars "with" and "without" and an arrow, a smooth line of "performance" against "power" with no units. Such a picture borrows the look of science to turn off the judgement of the person.
- **Each axis has a name, a unit and marked values.** A mark on a chart can be traced to a point on each axis.
- **Equal steps of the data are equal steps on the axis.** Do not space unequal intervals evenly, or equal ones unevenly, to get a smoother curve.
- **The size of each mark follows its number.** A bar for 500 next to a bar for 2,500 is one fifth as high, not one half. Check each mark against its label.
- **A chart has an author and a source.** Sign it, and name where the data come from and how they were collected: the people, the conditions, the time. A chart with no source makes the reader ask if the source exists. In a report, put the source under each chart.
- **A concept is not a chart.** Four curves that "show" four types of customer needs are a classification drawn as a graph to look exact. Ask: could the curve have a different shape, or lie higher? If the question has no meaning, there are no data. Write the idea as text or as a list.

## No pie charts

- **A pie chart forms an opinion and gives no data.** Its parts are judged by their area and their angle, at random angles and in random colors, and the same share looks larger or smaller by its place. In a pie with a 3D tilt, the front piece of five equal pieces looks the largest, also to a person who knows that they are equal.
- **Its clearness is an illusion.** "80% against 20%" as a pie feels understood, and the person does not ask: 80% of what, counted how (in money, in units), in which year, in which country? Numbers written out raise these questions.
- **Use bars or a table.** Bars on a common base line are compared by their length, which the eye does well. A table gives the exact values.
- **Two or three shares are a sentence**: "4 of the 12 bakeries sold less".
- **Do not bend data into a circle** because a circle looks good: options of a survey placed round a ring with blobs of color over them cannot be read.
- **A picture of a share must have the true share.** A clock face with one third filled says "20 minutes", also when no text says so.

## The scale

The scale alone changes what the data seem to say: the same two series look calm or wild by the stretch of the axis.

- **Bars start at zero.** A bar is read by its height. On an axis that starts at 500, a bar that is three times as high does not stand for a value that is three times as large.
- **A thick line at the bottom reads as zero.** If it is not zero, do not draw it.
- **Zero is not always needed.** Air pressure is always near 760. Then draw a line with no fill, no bars and no base line, "hung in the air", thus nobody compares heights.
- **The values of the axis are easy to see**, not pale and small at the edge. A steep fall on a chart can be a change of some percent.
- **The stretch: a mean slope of about 45°.** When a line is stretched in height, each part is steep and the differences between a rise and a fall cannot be seen. Choose the proportions so that the slopes of the line use the full range of angles; then the shape of the cycles shows (a fast rise and a slow fall).
- **"Too much" needs "compared with what".** To show that a value swung more than usual, show a longer period around it, not a taller chart.

## Lines and bars

How to build these from plain parts is in [Building a picture of data](./building-charts.md).


- **A line says that a value exists at each point between two marks.** A temperature at each moment is a line.
- **A value that belongs to a period is not a point**: visits in a day, sales in a month, medals of one Games. There is no "number of medals on 5 October at night". Joining such values with a line invents data, and a smoothed curve invents more: it shows a fall and a turn that nobody measured.
- **Draw a value of a period as a bar as wide as its period.** The width says "this value is for this whole day".
- **Narrower periods give a smoother shape by themselves.** One number for a month, four for its weeks, thirty for its days, and by the hour the bars become a curve. The curve is then true, because each of its points is a real value.
- **A few values with no trend are written as numbers.** Five numbers do not need a chart, and a change of one in a count that itself varies says nothing.
- **Lines between points of periods are permitted only to tell several series apart** on one chart: the line then marks which points belong together. Draw them straight, and do not smooth.

## Intervals

- **The choice of the interval can give each conclusion.** Six accidents in fifteen years, counted by five-year periods, show a fall; with the periods moved by two years, a rise; with a different length, no change. None of the three is true: the data are six dates.
- **With few events, show the events themselves** on a time line, and draw no trend.
- **For a conclusion, find more data** (the near misses, not only the accidents), not a better interval.
- **The same holds on a map.** Counts by districts of unequal size show the large districts as the worst, because more people live there. Use areas of equal size, or a value for each person (a rate).
- **Use the natural interval of the thing** (a day, a week, a season), and say in the report which you chose.

## Two series

- **Two series with different units do not share one pair of axes.** The scales are free, thus each crossing and each gap between the lines is made by you.
- **Put the charts one below the other with a common time axis.** The person still sees what happened at the same time.
- **One pair of axes only when the unit is the same**: two shares in percent, or several indexes as the change in percent from a common start. Then the comparison is honest ("fell three times as far").

## What to draw

- Axes with names, units and values, and a caption with the source and the date.
- Bars from zero, or a free line where zero has no meaning.
- Bars as wide as their period for counts in time.
- Bars or a table in the place of a pie.
- Two charts on a common time axis in the place of two scales on one chart.
