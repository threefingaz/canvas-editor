# Schematic diagrams

A schematic diagram shows how things are connected, not where they are: the lines of a transit network, the steps of a process, the parts of a system, the links between services. The transit map is the best-known case, and its lessons hold for each diagram of nodes and links.

## One question turns the values round

A good geographic map answers a million questions, and it is judged by how much it carries (see [Layers](./layers.md)). A schematic diagram is read in a hurry, in bad light, by a person with one question: how do I get from A to B. Here each thing that does not serve that question is in the way.

- **Name the one question of the diagram**, and remove each layer that does not answer it. Over the years a network map loses its streets and parks and keeps the lines, the stops and the river.
- **A diagram for specialists can be dense; one for the public cannot.** It must be clear to a person with no training who does not look closely.

## Leave the geography

- **Show the logical relations, as an electrical diagram does**: the place of an element need not be its physical place; what matters is that the connections read clearly and with one meaning.
- **Stretch the dense part and squeeze the empty part.** In the center, where the stops are close, move the lines apart until each name fits. At the edges, where the stops are far apart, put them close. A map that is true to the ground wastes most of its area on emptiness and cannot fit the names of the center at any size.
- **This is possible when the person does not see the way**, as in a tunnel: the ride is a jump from one stop to another. Where the person sees the way (a bus, a walk), the turns and the exact place of a stop matter, and the real ground must stay. A dependable form for that case: a true map of the near area, where each stop stands at its street, and a schematic part beyond it for where the lines go on; all stops inside the near area, only the main ones outside.

## Calm geometry

- **A small set of angles** (steps of 45° is the usual one) makes a network look simpler than it is.
- **The set of angles follows the place.** A ring line is a circle; a town whose streets run at a different angle gets a grid of its own. A grid that does not fit the town is disliked by the people who live there.
- **Turn and bend the whole picture to fit the format and the image of the place**: a coast drawn level, a long island drawn upright. Keep the proportions near enough that the place is still known.

## Keep what makes the place known

- **A river, a coast or a ring stays**, as a layer of its own that is clearly not a line of the network: a band of thin lines, a pale shape. It tells the person where they are in the diagram.
- **More of the ground can stay where it helps to find the way** (the grid of streets, the parks, the beaches), if it is pale enough to lie under the lines.

## Light marks for the common case

- **A plain stop is a small tick on the line**; a stop where lines meet is a ring. The tick points at its name, the ring stands out with no extra means, and the whole picture gets lighter. If each stop is a filled circle, a meeting of lines needs a heavy special mark. See "Do not spend strong means on weak things" in [Clutter](./clutter.md).
- **Routes that share one track are one line**, with the marks of the routes along it, and at each stop the list of the routes that stop there. A separate colored line for each of three routes that run together is too heavy.
- **Lines drawn with stripes or inner lines** vibrate and frighten. A line is one plain band.
- **When a name cannot be placed next to its stop without doubt, the layout is too dense.** An arrow from the name to the stop is a repair; a better layout needs none.

## The method follows the system

No set of rules fits each network or each diagram. A system has features of its own (lines that share a track, a ring, a river that cuts the town, a train that runs only at night), and the design must show them. Start from what is special about this system, not from the look of a famous diagram.

- **A diagram that people know is changed with care.** A network can double its lines over eighty years and keep the same grid of angles, the same ticks and the same rings; people then read the new one at once.

## What to draw

- Only the layers that answer the one question, and the landmark that says where the person is.
- Even spacing of the names, with the dense part stretched.
- A small set of angles that fits the place.
- Ticks for plain nodes and rings for the nodes where lines meet.
- One line for routes that run together.
