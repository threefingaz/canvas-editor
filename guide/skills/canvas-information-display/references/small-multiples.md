# Repeated elements

Take one small unit that shows one thing (a day, a project, a country, a flight) and repeat it for each thing, with the same design. The repeat forces a comparison, makes the differences stand out, and shows the range of the cases and how they relate. This file is about how to build such a unit, when the method fits, how to arrange the units, and why a set of them draws the eye.

## Fix the design, change the data

- **One design for each unit**: the same parts in the same places, the same scale, the same axes, the same view. Then each difference that the eye finds is a difference of the data.
- **Between neighbors, the least difference that has nothing to do with the subject.** A different crop, scale or color scheme in one unit is read as a fact about its content.
- **Explain the unit one time.** The first unit, or a key next to the set, has the axes, the units and the names of the marks. The others drop them. The person learns how the unit is built and then reads each one as a whole.
- **Each unit carries only what is its own.** No "day / night" in each cell of a forecast, no degree sign, no repeated axis labels. This is the common part outside the brackets.
- **One value, not a range, where the person compares.** "+21" is compared at a look; "+20…22" is not. Each person knows that a forecast is not exact.
- **A background color for a value** (the temperature of the day, the health of a project) lets the eye catch a change across the set at one look. Leave no divider lines between the cells, thus small steps of the color are seen (see [Clutter](./clutter.md)).
- **No heavy dividers between the units.** A white bar between frames of a sequence is louder than the frames; the joins are seen with no bar.
- **Add the numbers.** A row of photos of a launch shows the motion; the time, the speed and the height in the corner of each frame would make it data. A unit with no numbers is a picture.
- **The large number in a unit is one that is worth comparing** (the days in flight, the people on board), not an id.
- **The name of a unit stands at the unit**, clearly nearer to its own chart than to the next one. A label in the middle between two charts is read by habit with the wrong one.
- **A mark has the same meaning in each unit of the set**: if a dashed line is the lockdown in the first chart, it is not the last year in the second.
- **Two colors that the person compares by the size of their marks have a similar lightness**; else the lighter one looks smaller.

## A small chart for each thing

- **Many series on one chart are a tangle.** Give each series its own small chart, with the others drawn pale behind it. The person sees how this country stands against the rest, and finds it by the alphabet.
- **The shape of each small chart can be compared from far**: a sharp fall, a slow one, a weekly wave, a step in two stages. Odd cases show at once.
- **Lesser measures need less.** After one full chart with axes and labels, the charts of the related measures below it can be bare lines on the same time axis.

## When it fits, and when it does not

- **It fits when the shape of each unit, or its value, is what the person compares.**
- **It does not fit when the person needs the detail inside one unit.** Small maps of bus routes side by side fail twice: each map shows a different part of the town, thus the units have no common form; and with a common part of the town the routes get too small, and their shapes carry nothing, because the person needs the streets. Do not draw routes in this way.
- **It does not fit when the unit hides what the person judges**: live previews of filters in small squares do not show the detail of the picture.
- Ask: does the person learn something from the difference between two units? If not, the repeat is decoration.

## The place of a unit can carry meaning

The units can be more than a row. Their arrangement can itself be data.

- **By a law**: the table of the chemical elements. Each cell has its inner design, explained one time; the row and the column of a cell say how the element behaves.
- **Along a path**: photos of a skier at each turn of the slope, placed on the line of the run. The density of the frames shows the speed.
- **In a structure**: the matches of a tournament, each as a small picture of how it went, frame by frame. Align each match to its winner, not to the middle of the two matches before it: then the way of each player is one straight line through the table.
- **On a map**: a small fixed arrangement of the weather values, placed at each weather station.
- **By the time, with a second reading down the unit**: a row of flights by date, each a column with its photo, its rocket to one scale and its text. The person reads across for the history and down for one flight. A thing that grows out of its cell (a rocket that is taller than the others) is permitted to cross it; that is the point.

## A set draws the eye

Before a thing can be explained, the person must look at it. Many things with some properties in common and some that differ draw attention by themselves: a shelf of cups of one shape with different patterns holds the eye, where one cup does not.

- **To show a collection, fix the frame and let the content vary**: the same angle, size and background for each item. The series is then seen as a series, and each item becomes interesting through its neighbors.
- **The effect is strong enough to carry a joke**: the same photo with a different label under each copy makes people compare the copies.
- **Use it for content that is worth the look.** The effect draws attention to the set; it does not add information where the units have none.

## What to draw

- Units of one design, with the explanation at the first one only.
- A small chart for each series, the others pale behind it.
- A value as a background color, with no lines between the cells.
- An arrangement of the units that says something: by time, on a map, in the structure of the thing.
- No repeat where the person needs the inside of one unit.
